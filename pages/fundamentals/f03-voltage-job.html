F03 — Voltage’s job: how current changes

Torque comes mostly from current. So the next question is: how do we influence current? The practical answer is: we apply voltage.

On this page 1) Voltage “pushes” current 2) Voltage budget: where voltage gets “spent” 3) Why higher speed needs more voltage Next

1) Voltage “pushes” current

A motor phase winding is not just a wire. Electrically, it behaves like:

  • Resistance (R): causes a voltage drop when current flows.
  • Inductance (L): resists fast changes of current.
  • Back-EMF: a “generated voltage” that increases with speed and fights your applied voltage.
Beginner takeaway:
Voltage is the tool that lets you change current and hold it at the level you want.

2) Voltage budget: where voltage gets “spent”

When you apply voltage to a motor phase, that voltage is “spent” in three places:

  1. Back-EMF (speed-related):
    When the motor spins, it generates its own voltage. That generated voltage opposes your applied voltage. Faster motor → larger back-EMF.
  2. Resistance drop (I·R):
    The copper windings have resistance. Current flowing through them causes a voltage drop. More current (heavy load) → more voltage needed.
  3. Inductive drop (L·dI/dt):
    Inductance resists changes in current. If you want current to rise or fall quickly (fast torque change), you need extra voltage for that “kick”.
One simple sentence to remember:
Required voltage is used to overcome back-EMF (speed), I·R (load current), and L·dI/dt (fast current changes).

3) Why higher speed needs more voltage

At low speed, back-EMF is small, so it’s relatively easy to push current.

At high speed, back-EMF becomes large. It is like the motor is “pushing back” electrically. To maintain the same current (and torque), you often need more applied voltage.

This is why drives have a voltage limit. When speed gets high enough, you can hit the voltage limit even if you still want more torque. (We will explain that “voltage wall” and field weakening in F08.)
