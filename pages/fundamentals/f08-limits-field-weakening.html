F08 — Limits and field weakening (the high-speed “voltage wall”)

In real motor drives you cannot “push harder forever”. You hit limits: current, voltage, heating, and mechanics. This page explains those limits and why field weakening exists at high speed.

On this page 1) The four limits (simple) 2) The high-speed voltage wall 3) Field weakening (why less torque can mean more top speed) Next

1) The four limits (simple)

A motor drive is always constrained by at least four limits:

  1. Current limit:
    Too much current overheats windings and power electronics. Controllers reduce voltage/duty (or reduce torque request) to keep current safe.
  2. Voltage limit:
    The inverter has a maximum voltage it can produce (set by the DC bus and switching). In SVM, this appears as the hexagon boundary: you cannot command a voltage vector outside it.
  3. Thermal limit:
    Even if current is “allowed” for a short moment, you may not be allowed to hold it continuously. Continuous torque is usually lower than peak torque.
  4. Mechanical limit:
    Bearings, rotor stress, vibration, and safety requirements limit maximum speed and torque.
Beginner takeaway:
“Maximum magnitude all the time” is not control — it’s how you hit limits quickly.

2) The high-speed voltage wall

At high speed, the motor generates a large back-EMF. Back-EMF acts like an opposing voltage.

Required voltage = back-EMF + I·R + L·dI/dt
As speed rises, back-EMF rises, so the required voltage rises.

Eventually you reach the inverter’s maximum available voltage. That is the voltage wall. Past this wall, you cannot increase voltage magnitude anymore even if you want more torque.

3) Field weakening (why less torque can mean more top speed)

This is the key confusion most beginners have:

Below the voltage wall:
More torque → more acceleration → higher speed (over time). ✅
At the voltage wall:
You are no longer limited by “how much torque you want”.
You are limited by “you cannot apply more voltage”. 🚧

Back-EMF depends on speed and on the motor’s magnetic field strength (flux). Stronger field → stronger back-EMF at the same speed.

So when you hit the voltage wall but still want higher speed, the controller can reduce flux. That reduces back-EMF and frees voltage headroom, so the field can rotate faster.

Trade-off:
Lower flux → lower torque capability (you can’t push as hard).
But you can reach a higher top speed because you removed the voltage wall.

Good mental model: field weakening is like shifting into a higher gear. Higher possible speed, but less torque at the wheels.
